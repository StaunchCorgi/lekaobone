Many LGBTQ+ affirming therapists rely on Psychology Today to connect with new clients. It is one of the best-known therapist directories available, but relying on a directory alone means building your online presence on a platform you do not control.

A Psychology Today profile is a useful starting point. It should not necessarily be the final destination for your practice online.

Your own website gives potential clients a place to understand your values, approach and personality before they decide whether to contact you.

## Psychology Today is useful—but limited

Psychology Today can help therapists:

- Appear in front of people actively searching for support.
- Establish a basic professional presence.
- List specialties, availability and contact details.
- Benefit from the directory's existing visibility.

Those benefits matter, particularly for a new private practice.

The limitation is that every therapist is presented within the same platform, surrounded by other profiles offering similar services.

A potential client can move from your profile to another therapist with one click.

## A directory profile does not belong to you

Your Psychology Today profile exists on someone else's platform.

That means you have limited control over:

- The layout and visual identity.
- How much information you can present.
- The client journey.
- Search-engine optimisation.
- Your content and educational resources.
- Changes to the platform's pricing or policies.

Your website, by comparison, is an online asset that belongs to your practice.

You decide how it looks, what it communicates and what visitors should do next.

## Your website creates space for your real voice

Therapy is deeply personal. Choosing a therapist involves more than comparing qualifications.

Potential clients may want to understand:

- How you describe your therapeutic approach.
- Which communities you work with.
- Whether your practice feels genuinely inclusive.
- What they can expect during their first session.
- How you approach identity, relationships and mental health.
- Whether they are likely to feel safe speaking openly with you.

A short directory profile cannot always communicate these details adequately.

A thoughtfully designed website gives you enough space to explain your work without forcing it into a standard template.

## Why this matters to LGBTQ+ clients

For many LGBTQ+ people, contacting a therapist can carry additional uncertainty.

A potential client may quietly wonder:

- Will I be understood?
- Is this therapist genuinely affirming?
- Will I need to educate them about my identity?
- Can I speak openly about my relationships?
- Is this practice safe for transgender and non-binary clients?

Your website cannot answer every personal concern, but it can reduce uncertainty.

Inclusive language, thoughtful imagery, clear service descriptions and transparent information can help visitors understand whether your practice may be a suitable fit.

This is not about displaying a rainbow graphic and calling a website inclusive. It is about communicating care, competence and understanding throughout the entire experience.

## Your website can answer questions before someone contacts you

People may hesitate to contact a therapist when they do not know what will happen next.

Your website can answer common questions such as:

- Do you offer online or in-person therapy?
- Which locations do you serve?
- What happens during the first session?
- What are your fees?
- Do you accept insurance?
- Which age groups do you work with?
- What concerns do you commonly support?
- How can someone request a consultation?

Making this information easy to find removes unnecessary friction.

A visitor who understands your process may feel more prepared to make contact.

## A website can help your practice appear in Google

A directory profile can appear in search results, but it mainly helps build the directory's overall online presence.

Your own website gives your practice the opportunity to build independent visibility.

For example, an LGBTQ+ affirming therapist could publish useful resources addressing searches such as:

- How to find an LGBTQ+ affirming therapist.
- What to expect from gender-affirming therapy.
- Therapy for coming-out anxiety.
- LGBTQ+ couples counselling.
- How to support a transgender teenager.
- Online LGBTQ+ therapy in a specific city.

Each genuinely helpful page creates another possible entry point into your practice.

This process takes time. SEO is not an instant source of clients, but it can become a long-term source of relevant website traffic.

## Your website helps people remember you

Directory pages often look similar.

A distinctive website makes it easier for visitors to remember:

- Your name.
- Your practice.
- Your visual identity.
- Your specialisations.
- The way you made them feel.

That recognition matters because people do not always contact a therapist during their first search.

Someone may visit several profiles, think about the decision and return days later. A clear and memorable website gives them somewhere to return.

## Psychology Today and your website should work together

This is not an argument for deleting your Psychology Today profile.

The strongest approach may be to use both:

1. Psychology Today helps someone discover you.
2. Your profile links them to your website.
3. Your website explains your approach in greater depth.
4. Clear information builds confidence.
5. The visitor requests a consultation.

The directory provides reach. Your website provides depth, trust and ownership.

## What should an LGBTQ+ therapist website include?

A useful therapist website does not need dozens of pages.

A strong starting structure may include:

- A clear homepage.
- An about page.
- Individual service or specialty pages.
- A fees and frequently asked questions page.
- A contact or consultation request page.
- Privacy information.
- Helpful educational articles.
- Clear information about online and in-person availability.

The goal is not to create the largest website. It is to create the clearest and most reassuring experience for the people you want to support.

## Your website is a long-term practice asset

Social platforms, directories and advertising channels can all be useful.

However, they should lead people toward an online presence you control.

Your website becomes the central home for your:

- Professional identity.
- Services.
- Educational content.
- Search visibility.
- Contact process.
- Future marketing campaigns.

As your practice evolves, the website can evolve with it.

## Final thoughts

Psychology Today can help potential clients discover your profile, but discovery is only one part of choosing a therapist.

Your own website gives people the time and space to understand who you are, how you work and whether your practice feels right for them.

For LGBTQ+ affirming therapists, that clarity can be especially meaningful.

A website should not simply prove that your practice exists. It should help the right people feel comfortable taking the next step.
